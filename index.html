    Anticoagulation in nephrotic syndrome — when to start & stop
    Membranous nephropathy carries the highest thromboembolic risk of all nephrotic glomerular diseases
    Venous thromboembolism (VTE) often occurs within the first 6 months of diagnosis. Risk increases as serum albumin falls. The decision to anticoagulate is based on estimating thromboembolic risk vs. bleeding risk.
    WHEN TO START (prophylactic)
    • Serum albumin < 25 g/L — strongest single predictor; VTE risk rises sharply below this threshold[citation:19]
    • Serum albumin 25–30 g/L — consider if additional risk factors present (proteinuria > 8 g/d, BMI > 35, prior VTE, immobilization, genetic thrombophilia)
    • Proteinuria > 10 g/d — independent risk factor
    • First 6 months after diagnosis — highest-risk window
    • Use the online risk tool (UNC bleed risk calculator) to estimate patient-specific bleeding risk before starting[citation:18]
    • Decision rule: Anticoagulate when estimated VTE risk exceeds estimated bleeding risk[citation:1]
    WHEN TO STOP
    • After complete remission — proteinuria < 0.3 g/d and albumin normalized (> 35 g/L)
    • After partial remission with stable albumin — proteinuria < 3.5 g/d and albumin > 30 g/L for ≥ 3 months
    • If bleeding occurs — immediately stop, reverse if indicated, reassess risk-benefit
    • If a thromboembolic event occurred — anticoagulation is generally continued indefinitely (not stopped after remission)[citation:19]
    • Typical prophylactic duration — 3–6 months, or until remission of nephrotic syndrome (mean ~4 months in one study)[citation:2]

    AgentDoseWhen to useCautions
    Warfarin Target INR 2–3
    Start 5 mg/d, titrate    		 Preferred for treatment of confirmed VTE (bridged with IV heparin initially). Long-term experience. Requires frequent INR monitoring because warfarin-protein binding fluctuates with changing albumin[citation:1]. More bleeding events vs. apixaban in one NS study (17% vs. 3.3%)[citation:2]. Drug interactions.
    LMWH
    (e.g., enoxaparin)    		 Prophylactic: 40 mg SC daily
    Treatment: 1 mg/kg SC BID    		 Prophylaxis during transient high-risk events. Bridge to warfarin for confirmed VTE. Higher doses may be needed due to antithrombin III urinary loss[citation:1]. Reduce dose if CrCl < 30. Avoid in kidney failure. Monitor anti-Xa if very high BMI or severe hypoalbuminemia.
    Apixaban (DOAC) 2.5 mg BID (prophylaxis)
    5 mg BID (treatment)    		 Growing evidence as alternative to warfarin for prophylaxis[citation:2]. One study showed no VTE events and significantly fewer bleeds vs. warfarin (3.3% vs. 17%, p=0.003). Not formally recommended by KDIGO — "not systematically studied in NS; pharmacokinetics affected by hypoalbuminemia and urinary loss."[citation:1][citation:18]
    Rivaroxaban (DOAC) 20 mg daily with food Same caveats as apixaban. 66% urinary clearance; 92–95% albumin-bound — effects of hypoalbuminemia unknown. Higher urinary clearance than apixaban (66% vs. 27%). Less favorable pharmacokinetic profile in NS.
    Aspirin 75–100 mg daily Insufficient to prevent VTE[citation:7]. May be used for arterial thromboprophylaxis in select patients when VTE risk is lower. Not recommended as sole agent for VTE prophylaxis in NS.
    DOACs in nephrotic syndrome — key limitation

    DOACs are heavily albumin-bound (apixaban 92–94%, rivaroxaban 92–95%) and are lost in urine when proteinuria is severe. The effects of hypoalbuminemia on drug dosing have not been studied. Despite favorable case reports, KDIGO 2021 and KDOQI commentary state these drugs cannot be generally recommended in nephrotic patients until pharmacokinetic studies are performed[citation:1][citation:18]. Warfarin remains the anticoagulant of choice for confirmed VTE in MN.

    Side effects & monitoring
    Immediate / infusion reactions
    • Infusion reactions — 13% of patients; fever, chills, hypotension, bronchospasm, urticaria; usually occur 15–90 min after start[citation:14][citation:15]
    • Anaphylaxis — 5.8% (rare but serious); have epinephrine, hydrocortisone, antihistamine ready[citation:14]
    • Management: Stop infusion, give IV antihistamine + hydrocortisone, restart at half rate if mild
    Delayed / long-term
    • Infection — most common AE; 40.6% overall in one cohort, 17.6% in pMN specifically. Respiratory infections most common (28.3%)[citation:14]
    • Hypogammaglobulinemia — 28.6% with long-term use; monitor IgG levels[citation:14]
    • Neutropenia — 5.5%; late-onset neutropenia can occur weeks after infusion[citation:14]
    • Interstitial lung disease / pneumonitis — rare (0.1%) but serious; consider if new hypoxia/dyspnea[citation:14][citation:20]
    • Hepatitis B reactivation — screen all patients (HBsAg, anti-HBc) before treatment
    Elderly patients (≥ 60 years) — special caution
    • ▸ Higher risk of infection due to age-related immune senescence + B-cell depletion + hypoalbuminemia[citation:3]
    • ▸ Low-dose, long-course regimen preferred — e.g., 375 mg/m² × 4 weekly doses rather than 1g × 2[citation:3]
    • ▸ Eosinophil percentage drop at 1 month may predict infection risk better than CD19+ B-cell count[citation:3]
    • ▸ Monitor closely for infection in the first 3–6 months after RTX
    Rituximab non-responder · stepwise algorithm
    Defined as: no proteinuria reduction > 25% at 6 months AND anti-PLA2R not falling OR rising
    1
    Confirm non-response & exclude mimics

    Verify adherence, rule out infection, malignancy, NSAIDs, or superimposed diabetic nephropathy. Re-check anti-PLA2R, proteinuria, eGFR.

    → Re-biopsy if atypical course
    2
    Optimize rituximab exposure

    Check CD19/CD20 count. If B-cells repopulated, re-dose rituximab 1g × 2 (days 0, 14). Consider higher dose if incomplete depletion.

    → Re-dose RTX 1g × 2
    3
    Switch or combine mechanism

    Add CNI (tacrolimus 0.05–0.1 mg/kg/d) OR switch to cyclophosphamide + alternate-month steroids (Ponticelli).

    → TAC + RTX or CYC protocol
    4
    Third-line / rescue therapy

    If high risk & refractory: obinutuzumab (anti-CD20), daratumumab (anti-CD38), belimumab + RTX, or ACTH gel. Consider clinical trial enrollment.

    → Obinutuzumab / anti-CD38 / trial
    ⚠️ Important: Before declaring rituximab failure, exclude secondary MN (malignancy, infection, drug) and confirm adequate B-cell depletion. Rising anti-PLA2R with stable proteinuria may be early immunologic relapse — re-treat with RTX before proteinuria worsens.

    Prednisone in MN · Evidence summary
    ❌ Prednisone monotherapy is NOT recommended

    Cochrane 2014 + KDIGO 2021/2025: corticosteroids alone do NOT increase remission rates.
